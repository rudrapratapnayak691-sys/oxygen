Primary Oxygen Source: Water Electrolysis

On the International Space Station, the main source of breathable oxygen is the Oxygen Generation Assembly (OGA). It splits water into oxygen and hydrogen using electricity from the solar arrays.

The Reaction

2 H₂O → 2 H₂ + O₂

An electric current passes through purified water in a stack of electrolysis cells. Oxygen is released into the cabin atmosphere. Hydrogen is either vented overboard or sent to a Sabatier reactor that combines it with captured CO₂ to make more water.

  • Maximum production: ~9.25 kg O₂ per day (supports up to ~11 crew)
  • Typical operation: sized for the current crew of 6–7
  • Russian Elektron units perform the same function with alkaline electrolysis
  • Water comes from the Water Recovery System (recycled humidity, hygiene, and urine)

Oxygen Storage & Air Circulation

Generated oxygen is mixed into the cabin air and distributed by fans. High-pressure tanks provide reserves and make up for leaks or EVA losses.

Cabin Atmosphere

Maintained near Earth sea-level conditions:

  • Total pressure: ~14.7 psi (101 kPa)
  • Oxygen: ~21% (partial pressure ~3.1 psi)
  • Nitrogen: ~78% (for fire safety and comfort)
  • Humidity controlled to keep condensation low

High-Pressure Storage

U.S. High-Pressure Gas Tanks store oxygen at up to ~2,700 psi. Each can hold roughly 100 kg of O₂ — enough for many days of crew use if generation stops.

Nitrogen tanks maintain total pressure when oxygen is added or air is lost.

Circulation

Fans and ducting keep air moving so oxygen reaches every module and CO₂ does not pool. Inter-module ventilation is continuous. Filters remove particulates and some contaminants before air returns to the habitat volume.

Carbon Dioxide Removal

Crew exhalation produces CO₂ that must be removed continuously. On the ISS this is done primarily by the Carbon Dioxide Removal Assembly (CDRA) using zeolite molecular sieves, plus Russian systems.

How CDRA Works

Cabin air is forced through beds of zeolite that adsorb CO₂. Once a bed is saturated, it is heated and the CO₂ is desorbed — either vented or sent to the Sabatier reactor.

The Sabatier reaction recovers some oxygen:

CO₂ + 4 H₂ → CH₄ + 2 H₂O

Water is recycled into the electrolysis loop. Methane is currently vented. This closes roughly half of the oxygen loop.

Backup Scrubbers

Lithium hydroxide (LiOH) canisters chemically absorb CO₂ for emergency use. They are not regenerative and must be replaced.

Russian Vozdukh and newer amine-based systems provide additional capacity and redundancy.

Target CO₂ partial pressure is kept well below 3 mmHg to avoid headaches and performance issues.

Monitoring & Crew Maintenance

Sensors & Analyzers

The Major Constituent Analyzer (MCA) and dedicated sensors continuously measure oxygen, nitrogen, CO₂, and total pressure. Data is available to the crew and to ground controllers in real time.

Routine Maintenance

Astronauts replace filters, swap Orbital Replacement Units (ORUs), clean sensors, and verify fan operation. Many components are designed for on-orbit replacement so the habitat never loses life-support capability.

Trace Contaminant Control

Activated charcoal beds, catalytic oxidizers, and other filters remove volatile organic compounds and other trace gases that can build up from equipment and crew activity.

Equipment Failures & Emergency Reserves

Multiple independent backups ensure breathable air even if primary systems fail.

High-Pressure Tanks

Stored oxygen and nitrogen can repressurize the cabin and supply breathing gas for days to weeks depending on crew size and leak rate.

Solid Fuel Oxygen Generators (SFOG)

Chemical “candles” that release oxygen when ignited. One canister provides roughly one person-day of oxygen. Used only in emergencies.

LiOH Canisters

Emergency CO₂ scrubbers. Simple, reliable, and non-regenerative. Carried in sufficient quantity for contingency scenarios.

Portable Breathing Apparatus

Quick-don oxygen masks give individual crew members immediate breathable gas during fires, toxic leaks, or rapid depressurization while the habitat systems are restored.

Current Spacecraft vs Future Concepts

Current (ISS & Near-Term)

  • Water electrolysis (OGA / Elektron)
  • ~50% oxygen recovery via Sabatier
  • Zeolite and amine CO₂ removal
  • High-pressure tank reserves + chemical backups
  • Regular resupply of water and spare parts
  • Advanced OGA (AOGA) upgrades launching for higher reliability

Future Concepts

  • Higher loop closure (Bosch or methane pyrolysis → ~100% oxygen recovery)
  • Static vapor-feed or magnetohydrodynamic electrolysis (fewer moving parts)
  • More maintainable, longer-life hardware with less crew time
  • Solid-oxide systems suitable for lunar/Martian ISRU
  • Reduced mass and power for deep-space transit
  • Still relies on physical/chemical systems — no dependence on growing crops

Future habitats will push toward closed-loop regenerative systems so that less mass must be launched from Earth, while retaining robust emergency reserves.
